Your manager won’t remember everything you did this year.  
HR won’t track your contributions. Neither will leadership.  
But if you document it, you control the narrative.  
Here’s what to track 👇  
⸻  
  
📊 Keep a “brag sheet” of every win  
Every project delivered. Every problem solved. Every metric improved.  
Date it. Quantify it. Save it in a running doc.  
When performance review comes, you have receipts.  
Your manager has a vague memory. You have data.  
  
📧 Save important emails and messages  
Praise from clients. Positive feedback from teammates.  
Approvals from leadership.  
Screenshot Slack messages. Forward emails to a personal folder.  
  
When someone questions your contributions, you have proof.  
Memory fades. Documentation doesn’t.  
  
🗂️ Track conversations with your manager  
After every 1:1, send a quick recap email:  
“Thanks for discussing X. Just to confirm, we agreed on Y. Let me know if I missed anything.”  
  
This creates a paper trail.  
If priorities shift or blame starts flying, you’re protected.  
  
🎯 Document goals at the start of the year  
Write down what your manager expects. Get it in writing.  
When review time comes, you can say:  
“Here’s what we agreed on in January. Here’s how I delivered.”  
  
No ambiguity. No moving goalposts.

For a long time, I thought doing good work was enough.  
  
But I realised this —  
  
If you don’t track your work, people forget it.  
Even you forget it.  
  
I wasn’t bad at my job.  
I was just invisible.  
Especially during 1:1s and appraisals.  
  
So I started tracking everything in a quiet, simple way.  
  
Here’s what I do:  
  
1. Weekly work log:  
  
Every Friday, I write down:  
– What I did  
– What I solved  
– What changed because of it  
  
2. Outcome over effort:  
  
Not “Created dashboard”  
But “Created dashboard → reduced reporting time by 3 hours a week”  
  
3. Save feedback:  
  
Any Slack message, email, or meeting praise — I save it. These are the things that help you tell your story later.  
  
4. Monthly reflection:  
  
At the end of every month, I write a short summary for myself. Sometimes I share it with my manager, sometimes I don’t. But it’s always ready when I need it.  
  
5. Keep the language clear  
No fancy words. Just:  
– “I helped…”  
– “I improved…”  
– “I took ownership…”  
  
This isn’t about bragging.  
It’s about not being forgotten.  
  
If you’re serious about growth — start tracking your work.  
  
It will change how people see you.  
And how you see yourself.

Background checks feel scary when your exit wasn’t clean.  
  
But remember:  
BGV is a compliance process — not a revenge tool.  
  
Here’s how to protect yourself 👇  
  
1️⃣ Understand What’s Actually Verified  
  
Most companies verify:  
  
• Employment dates  
• Designation  
• Reporting manager  
• Exit type (voluntary/involuntary)  
  
They usually don’t share detailed performance gossip.  
  
Fear grows in imagination.  
  
2️⃣ Control Your Narrative Early  
  
If asked about your exit, keep it neutral:  
  
“The role expectations evolved, and it wasn’t a long-term alignment. I’ve since strengthened X and Y.”  
  
No blaming.  
No emotional storytelling.  
  
Confidence reduces suspicion.  
  
3️⃣ Prepare Alternative References  
  
If your direct manager was toxic:  
  
• Use previous managers  
• Cross-functional leads  
• Senior colleagues  
• Clients  
  
Strong references balance risk.  
  
4️⃣ Keep Documentation  
  
Save:  
  
• Offer letter  
• Relieving letter  
• Salary slips  
• Appraisals  
• Appreciation emails  
  
Facts > feelings.  
  
5️⃣ Stay Consistent Across Platforms  
  
Your resume, LinkedIn, and interview story must align.  
  
Inconsistency triggers red flags faster than a tough exit.  
  
6️⃣ Don’t Lie  
  
Short-term cover-ups destroy long-term credibility.  
  
Companies reject dishonesty more than performance gaps.  
  
Final Truth  
  
A toxic manager cannot destroy your career  
unless you panic and lose composure.  
  
Clarity + documentation + calm framing  
makes BGV manageable.  
  
Your next employer cares more about who you are now  
than who disliked you before.

Most people think they are the problem… but sometimes, it’s your manager.  
  
1️⃣ Everything is urgent, all the time  
If your boss creates fake urgency daily, it’s not your fault. It’s poor management.  
  
2️⃣ Feedback only when you’re wrong  
If you only hear from them when you mess up, you’re not bad at your job. They just don’t know how to lead.  
  
3️⃣ Constantly changing expectations  
If targets keep changing, you’re not confused… they are.  
  
4️⃣ They take credit, you take blame  
If your work gets no recognition but mistakes get highlighted, that’s not performance issue… that’s toxic leadership.  
  
5️⃣ You feel anxious before talking to them  
If you feel fear instead of comfort, it’s not “professional pressure”… it’s a bad boss.  
  
If your manager is doing this… YOU are not the problem. Your manager is.

is it okay to leave after 6 months or 1 year of my job?” 🚪  
  
This is a really tricky situation that many people face in their first job. Maybe the work environment is toxic, or you’re not learning anything, or you got a much better offer, or the company culture is just not what you expected. But you’re worried - will leaving so early look bad on my resume?  
  
Will future employers reject me for job hopping?  
  
Here’s the honest truth - leaving a job after 6 months to 1 year is not ideal, but sometimes it’s necessary and justified.  
  
If you’re in a genuinely bad situation - toxic workplace, no growth, mental health suffering, or you have a significantly better opportunity - then yes, you should consider leaving.  
  
Your career and well-being matter more than what looks good on paper.  
  
I’m breaking down when it’s okay to leave early and when you should stick it out. If it’s just minor inconveniences or you’re bored, try to complete at least 1-2 years.  
  
But if it’s affecting your growth, health, or you have a life-changing opportunity, don’t force yourself to stay. When you interview next, be honest about why you left - focus on positive reasons like seeking growth, better opportunities, or skill development.  
  
One early exit won’t ruin your career, but don’t make it a pattern. Choose your next company more carefully and try to stay longer there! 💼

The only talk that remains secret is the one that you didn't say!! Keep this in mind

Most career damage doesn’t happen because of incompetence…  
It happens because of careless conversations.  
Be smart about where you speak.  
Comfort zones in office are rarely confidential zones

Well, there are **three places in your office where you should never ever share anything confidential or secret**.

- **Firstly is your washroom.** An employee once told a colleague in the washroom that they were taking a sick leave the following day to combine it with a Monday public holiday for a four-day break, rather than using privileged leave. Unfortunately, their manager was in one of the vestibules and overheard everything. In the subsequent team meeting, the manager announced that no sick leaves would be permitted the next day due to a payroll cutoff, preventing any unplanned leave.
- **Secondly is the smoking zone.** This is a place where people relax and vent their frustrations, often leading them to overshare. What feels like personal bonding can quickly become office news within hours.
- **Third is in front of the wrong audience.** You may share something with a colleague while someone from another team is sitting or standing nearby. While it may seem harmless, you do not know who that person is close to; they might be close to your manager, allowing your secret to travel.

Always remember that at workplaces, **"even the walls have ears"**.

**it doesn't matter whether you are actually doing hard work or not, but showing that you are doing hard work matters the most**.

There used to be a guy in our office who would arrive at 1:00 PM and would often disappear for two hours during lunch. However, when it was time to leave the office—especially when the boss was leaving—he would be at his desk talking loudly about work. Even if the official exit time was 6:00 PM, he would go home and send compiled work to the boss at 8:00 PM. He created an image that he was always working both before and after office hours.

Even if he wasn't active in the group chat all week, he would definitely be active on Sunday to show that he works even on his day off. He projected the image of being that one employee who is **always working, always answering, and taking ownership**.

The lesson is that doing hard work is not as necessary as **showing that you are doing hard work**. You should **shout about your work from the rooftops**; if you arrive early or stay late, post a social media story about how much you "love your work". Doing this will ensure you get your appraisal quickly.

Never join without reading these seven points:

1. **Offer Letter Clauses:** Ensure the salary structure, probation rules, and working hours are all clearly defined.
2. **CTC vs. In-Hand Breakdown:** Do not just be happy hearing a figure like "5 LPA" (5 Lakhs Per Annum); check exactly how much you will receive in-hand.
3. **Bond Agreement:** Beware of "bond traps" where you are tied down for one or two years in the name of training.
4. **Notice Period:** Check if the notice period is 30, 90, or 180 days so you do not find yourself stuck later.
5. **Job Role and Responsibilities:** Understand what your actual role will be—whether it involves real skill development or just basic Excel work.
6. **Leave Policy:** Details regarding casual leaves and sick leaves should be clearly mentioned.
7. **Exit Policy:** A very common clause allows a company to fire you easily, while making it difficult for you to leave.

**Essential Documents to Collect Before Leaving a Job**

To avoid major HR or salary issues later, you should collect the following documents before switching jobs:

- **Experience Letter:** Official proof of employment; without it, background verification or your start date at a new job may be delayed.
- **Relieving Letter:** Confirms you left on good terms, which is required for onboarding at some companies.
- **Last 3 Months' Salary Slips:** Necessary for background checks and salary negotiations; keep both soft and hard copies.
- **Form 16 / Tax Documents:** Essential for tax filing and switching payrolls.
- **PF & UAN Details:** Required for transferring or withdrawing provident funds.
- **Increment / Promotion Letters:** Useful for proving career growth, verification, or loan applications.
- **Clearance Letter (if applicable):** Proves you have returned company assets and cleared all dues.
