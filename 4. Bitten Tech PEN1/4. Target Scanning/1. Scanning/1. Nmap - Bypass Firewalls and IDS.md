### Break it down

`-D` = **Decoy scan**.

`RND:16` = Nmap generates **16 random decoy addresses**.

So, conceptually, the target may see something like:

The idea is that the target's logs may contain traffic appearing to come from multiple IP addresses, making it harder to immediately identify which source is the real scanner.

### Why use it?

Primarily for **testing/understanding IDS/IPS and source-attribution defenses** in authorized environments.

For **OSCP**, though, don't prioritize this. You should know what `-D` does, but it's much more important to master:

### One important limitation

Decoys **do not make you magically anonymous**. They can be ineffective or even suspicious depending on the network, routing, logging, and IDS/IPS. Also, Nmap notes that decoy scanning can have side effects and isn't appropriate in every environment.

**Easy memory:**

> `-D` → **Decoy** → make the scan appear to originate from multiple addresses.


Sure — the key is understanding **what Nmap actually does with decoys**.

With:

Nmap creates packets that **appear to come from multiple source IP addresses**.

Conceptually:

The target may therefore see traffic such as:

instead of seeing only:

### But here's why it isn't anonymity

The packets still have to travel through the network.

For example, if you're scanning a machine on your local network, the network infrastructure can potentially observe that **your actual machine generated the traffic**.

Also, the target may be able to distinguish the real scanner through things like:

- Which decoy IPs actually exist
- Network routing behavior
- TCP/IP characteristics
- Responses coming back to the real machine
- Firewall/IDS logs
- Packet timing and patterns

So decoys are better understood as:

> **Obscuring the scanner's identity in the target's logs, not hiding your identity from the entire network.**
