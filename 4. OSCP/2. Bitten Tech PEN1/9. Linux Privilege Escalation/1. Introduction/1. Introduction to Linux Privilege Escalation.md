### **[Section 3: Operating Systems, Reconnaissance & Initial Access Workflows]**

If you have studied my "AD for Pentesting" course before, you will remember this diagram, which is also commonly seen on TryHackMe machines[3]. You will study the AD course later, but this diagram represents the overall methodology and workflow of a penetration testing engagement[3]. First, regarding your target that you plan to assess in a systematic and controlled manner, you must determine what it is built of—what tech stack, platform, and operating system it uses[3].

In the real world, 99% of the market share for operating systems used across servers consists of two systems: **Linux** and **Windows**[3]. Speaking of Linux, many web servers and polling systems run on Linux[3]. Why? First, because Linux is highly customizable[3]. Second, it is open source, which brings both customizability and robustness[3].

People often think viruses are built primarily for Windows, whereas Linux viruses are extremely rare[3]. Linux systems rarely get infected by viruses, which is why servers are heavily built on Linux—it is far more immune to viruses, and its bandwidth, memory management, and customizability are excellent[4]. You have complete flexibility to install services, make kernel-level changes, or customize the OS by building your own kernel[4].

Whether the target machine uses Linux or Windows, the pentesting workflow remains the same[4]:

1. **Reconnaissance**: Discover what the system is running, what services are open, and what ports are active (Web server, FTP, Mail, etc.) to move forward[4].
2. **Initial Compromise**: Identify a vulnerability and find an exploit (SQL injection, SSH port default credentials, weak passwords, etc.) and run the payload/exploit against the target[4][5].

### **[Section 4: Establishing a Foothold & Lower-Privilege Service Accounts]**

1. **Establish a Foothold**: Once you run the exploit, you might not get direct Remote Code Execution (RCE) immediately; RCE can take multiple steps[5]. However, establishing a foothold means gaining remote access to compromise the system initially[5].

Because services usually run under low-privilege service accounts, privilege levels matter[5]. We will examine how privilege levels work in Linux[5]. Once you establish a foothold, you have initial access to run commands on the machine (RCE or shell access), but under a low-privileged account—like a service account[5].

1. **Escalating Privileges**: Stage 4 is where we focus in this course[5]. We will study Stage 4 and all the techniques involved in achieving privilege escalation to elevate privileges across multiple levels[5][6]. I will explain all the tools and techniques in depth[6].

### **[Section 5: Post-Exploitation, Active Directory & Privilege Escalation Goals]**

Remaining post-exploitation phases like maintaining persistence are covered in my Active Directory course, as most enterprise environments are AD-based[7]. If you get the OSCP bundle, you get both the attack techniques and the AD module, covering all these steps end-to-end[7].

Now, let's discuss what this course specifically covers[7]. As mentioned, initial access does not grant direct root privileges[7]. For example, when you exploit a web application, you usually get a shell as `www-data`[7]. That is a low-privilege system service account[7][8]. It has no assigned home directory or interactive login shell, so your capabilities are severely limited[8]:

- You can read a few files, but you cannot move laterally to other systems[8].
- You cannot change user passwords or store persistent credentials[8].
- You cannot stop system services/programs or disable firewalls[8].

To overcome these restrictions, you must **escalate privileges**—meaning you elevate your permissions, rights, and role to a user with higher system access[8].

In Linux, our ultimate goal is to gain **root** access[8]. (In Windows privilege escalation, the ultimate goal is `NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM` or local administrator)[8][9].

### **[Section 6: Enumeration as the Key to Privilege Escalation]**

To perform privilege escalation, you must first perform thorough reconnaissance and enumeration on the target host—just as you enumerated open ports and services for initial access[9].

Once you have a foothold, you must conduct extensive internal enumeration to discover _how_ to escalate privileges and _which_ technique to apply[9]. **Enumeration is the absolute key to privilege escalation**[9].

I have explained the difference between Information Gathering and Enumeration in both this course and the Windows course[9]. Enumeration is critical because privilege escalation is rarely direct—it involves complex, multi-step techniques[9][10].

In this course, we will learn how to enumerate Linux systems, exploit misconfigurations, escalate privileges to root, understand network operations during post-exploitation, and manage connections/roles as we move forward[10].
