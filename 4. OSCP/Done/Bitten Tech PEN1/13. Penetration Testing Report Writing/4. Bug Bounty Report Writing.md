
# Bug Bounty Report Writing

> [!summary] Quick Recall
> **Report components (in order):** Title → Rating (CVSS) → Introduction → Description → Steps to Reproduce → PoC → Impact → Mitigation → Remediation → Additional Notes → References
>
> **Title formula:** *vuln type + impact + asset*
> **Tips:** Thoroughness · Simplicity · Neutrality

---

## 1. Understanding Audience Standards

- Plan your report according to the **organizational standards**.
- Many stakeholders will read what you write — other QAers, Developers, Support, Product Management, Documentation, Management, etc. It may become more important to **use less jargon and add more details**.
- If offshore testers or developers must read your Issue Reports, pay special attention **not to use confusing jargon or colloquialisms**.
- You may even be better off having **two different descriptions** of the bug — one for internal consumption, and one for customers.

---

## 2. Essential Components of a Report

### Title
Simple but clear; explains what the report is about in **one single line**. It should contain the **type of vulnerability**, the **potential impact**, and **what asset is concerned**.

| Quality | Examples |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Good** | `Open redirect + Stored XSS in profile lead to account takeover on www.example.com` <br> `[192.168.1.1] Public Jenkins instance leads to RCE` |
| ⚠️ **So so** | `XSS on www.example.com` <br> `PHP errors reveal webapp full path` |
| ❌ **Forget it** | `XSS` <br> `Local file inclusion` <br> `Critical bug on www.example.com` |

### Rating
- Take time to rate the issue in an obvious way.
  - **Too low** → there is a chance the sec team passes over it (though you could be happy if the bounty ends up higher than expected).
  - **Too high** → the sec team could think you overrated it to increase the bounty; they will notice, lower the rating, lower the bounty, and you will be disappointed.
- Try to provide a suitable **CVSS score**. Even if it's not perfect and context-dependent, it gives a good idea of the criticality of the issue from a technical point of view. Note that some platforms award **bonus points** for that.

### Introduction
A reminder of the title, a little more verbose, but **no technical details at all**. You can also write a quick explanation of the class of the vulnerability.

### Description
In a nutshell, the **full explanation** of the vulnerability. Name the variables and their values, provide endpoints and all conditions required to trigger the issue: **what, when, where, who**, etc. The whole everything.

### Steps to Reproduce
- Goal: help the team reproduce the bug easily. Give the whole process step by step using an **ordered list**, so you can reference any step at any moment.
- **Providing the response** is also good, to show the team the difference between a legit result and an unexpected behavior.

Example (as shown on the slide):
```
3. Connect to your account: https://www.example.com/login
4. Click on the "profile" tab
5. Enter value payload in the input input
6. repeat step 2
…
```

> [!tip] Burp Suite requests
> If you use a local proxy like **Burp Suite**, provide the request in an **HTTP code block**. It's very easy to reproduce the issue that way — simply copy/paste it back into the software, update the cookie or any auth token, and that's it. Simple and efficient.

### PoC (Proof of Concept)
Provide everything that can prove the bug.

> [!warning] Reports may be publicly disclosed in the future — **hide personal information** you want to keep private.

- **Screenshots and images** that you can quickly modify with an image editor to highlight payloads and data extracted. No need to be a great designer here.
- **Videos** are even better — they replay the whole drama that led you to this great report.

### Impact
- It's the job of the hacker to **prove the criticality** of the vulnerabilities they find.
- Do not boast about a high severity; just be practical and think of the **real impact**.
- State in points for multiple issues, **in order of severity**.
- Try to create a possible **scenario** showing the potential risks of the issue — but take care not to fall into the **"Hollywood syndrome"**.

### Mitigation
- Stay obvious and honest: if you think some technical details make the issue **very hard to exploit**, it's important to let the team know.
- For instance, an RCE that can only be triggered in January, between 12 and 2am, on a full moon night 🙂

### Remediation
- Do you have any idea how to solve the problem?
- This is greatly appreciated by companies; they will be happy to read your tips/recommendations. Remember that bug bounty is also about **learning (for both parties)**.

### Additional Notes
- Sometimes you have to provide small details that help the team better understand the issue — e.g., why it works most of the time but fails in a specific case.
- The faster they reproduce the issue → the faster your report is triaged → the faster you get paid :)

### References
Links to external resources:
- OWASP article
- Blog articles (GitHub, Medium)
- CVE
- Disclosed reports
- Real case studies or whatever can support your report

The goal is to help the team understand and fix the issue, but also to show them its criticality.

## 3. Tips for Writing a Good Report

### Thoroughness
Make sure you cover **every single step** someone would need to follow to reproduce your bug:
- Will they need to be **logged in** to see it?
- Will it only work in a **specific browser**, or is it blocked by a **content security policy**?
- Is it clear **which elements on a page** you are referring to?
- If you have doubts about any of these, walk through the steps yourself and see if any steps could be ambiguous.

### Simplicity
- Find a balance between thoroughness and complexity. While it may sometimes require a full page of steps to describe a bug, this is often not necessary.
- **Example:** reporting a **reflected XSS** may be as simple as providing a link and saying which browsers it will execute in. There's no reason to include a stack trace or history of the web if your bug can be demonstrated by clicking a link!

### Neutrality
- You're trying to properly convey the impact of the bug. But with monetary rewards involved, it can be difficult to provide an unbiased assessment of your bug's actual impact.
- **Just be honest!** Presenting your bug as worse than it actually is can lose trust with a company, and could even result in a **lower bounty**.

## 4. Bug Bounty Terminologies

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Security Team** | A team of individuals responsible for addressing security issues found in a product or service |
| **Finder** | Also known as hackers. Anyone who has investigated a potential security issue in some form of technology |
| **Report** | A Finder's description of a potential security vulnerability in a particular product or service |
| **Vulnerability** | A software bug that would allow an attacker to perform an action in violation of an expressed security policy |
| **Programs** | Security Teams may publish a Program and Program Policy designed to guide security research into a particular service or product. Private program participation is entirely optional and non-disclosable by default |
