# Report Writing Mechanics (OSCP)

> [!important] Why this matters
> Report writing is a **pass/fail** section of the OSCP. Even if you obtain every flag and score a full 100 points on Day 1, **no report = automatic failure**. OffSec allocates 24h for the labs and a *separate* 24h for reporting — and for many candidates the second 24h is harder than the first.

---

## Slide Summary (quick recall)

**Why document?**
- Penetration Testing is a *process*, not a deliverable — documentation is the eye to that process.
- PT makes you skilled; reporting makes you a *professional*.
- OffSec allocates 24h for labs + 24h for reporting. First 24h < Next 24h.
- All flags obtained can still fail with 0 points if no report.

**What to document?**
- **All** steps, commands, outputs, exploit code.
- Flag screenshots — both local and proof — with file contents **and** the system network address together.
- Exploit code: modified code (and *why*), URL to the script, shellcode generation commands.

**Where to document?**
- Official OffSec Report Template (or build your own).
- Bring all proofs gathered from the attacking machine. Show your MS Word skills.

**How to document?**
- Documentation is part of the process — use a note-making app (OneNote, KeepNote, CherryTree).
- Draft a rough report along the way. Copy all outputs. Save screenshots for every step.
- Target audience is a **technically competent reader**: detailed enough for a professional, concise enough for a stakeholder.
- Don't assume anything. No grammar/spelling mistakes. Stick to the point. As many screenshots as possible. Proofread 2–3 times.

**Submission rules [IMPORTANT]**
- Exam report is in **PDF** format.
- PDF file name: `OSCP-OS-XXXXX-Exam-Report.pdf`
- PDF archived into a `.7z` file (**NO password**): `OSCP-OS-XXXXX-Exam-Report.7z`
- Archive must be **no more than 200MB**.
- Upload the `.7z` to https://upload.offsec.com

## Introduction: The Non-Negotiable Role of Report Writing

You might solve the entire technical portion exceptionally fast — say, finishing in three hours, informing the proctor you have all the flags, and closing the session. But Day 2 is **report writing**. Everything you accomplished must be thoroughly documented in a proper, professional report detailing what you did during those 24 hours.

The proctor observed your session live, but the evaluator reviewing your submission was not there. You must systematically document the entire penetration test: which machines were assessed, what vulnerabilities were identified, how you escalated privileges to root/administrator, and how each system was compromised. **Without a report, clearing the exam is impossible** — even a full 100 points on Day 1 still requires a complete report to pass.

This is arguably more critical than the technical exploitation phase, because candidates frequently make avoidable reporting mistakes and fail regardless of how well their technical exam went.

---

## Why Documentation Matters: PT as a Business Deliverable

Penetration testing is a **systematic process** — a structured methodology executed by a skilled professional to evaluate how secure or vulnerable a target truly is. We deploy attacks, conduct active reconnaissance, probe entry vectors, and analyze architectural/design weaknesses using technical expertise and tooling.

As a tester you possess the technical capabilities, but the asset owner (the business, executive, or corporate client) is generally **not** technical. The purpose of testing is to identify vulnerabilities so the client can remediate them. You cannot simply hack a machine and walk away; you must explain your findings so they comprehend the business risk, because leadership must justify and allocate budget to fix those issues.

A penetration test is never finished merely by breaching systems. Technical exploitation is not the final deliverable — **the true deliverable is the written report**: a structured document with a clear table of contents, descriptive headings, exact commands, relevant screenshots, and visual evidence that both technical and non-technical stakeholders can digest.

Documentation is also essential for **re-testing**: when a developer patches an issue, you follow your exact documented steps to verify whether the vulnerability was properly resolved.

---

## Professional Accountability, Standards & Compliance

Technical skills make you *capable*; rigorous documentation makes you a *professional*. Anyone can root a device at home and call themselves a hacker. A professional can clearly articulate **what** was done, **how** it was executed, and maintain structured audit logs and reports within an enterprise database.

Regulatory standards require formal documentation. Organizations managing sensitive assets — corporate networks, payment card data (**PCI DSS**), or protected health information (**HIPAA**) — must adhere to strict compliance frameworks. Regulators require formal audit reports proving environments were independently tested and verified secure before authorization to process/store sensitive data.

---

## The 24-Hour Reporting Window & the Communication Hurdle

OffSec allocates a dedicated **24-hour reporting window**. While the practical phase lasts 24h (or longer in 48h / 4-day exams), reporting receives its own dedicated 24h because it is an essential skill.

Many technically proficient candidates can compromise systems effortlessly but struggle with written communication, grammar, vocabulary, and tailoring technical information to an audience.

Even if you recover every user flag (`local.txt`) and root flag (`proof.txt`) and achieve maximum points, **you receive a score of zero and fail immediately if you fail to submit a report**. The official exam guide explicitly states: no report equals automatic failure.

---

## What to Document: Walkthroughs & Command Outputs

In short: **document everything.** The most effective approach is documenting **progressively along the way** as you execute each step — like a structured Hack The Box / VulnHub walkthrough that follows a logical narrative.

- Document every individual step in sequence without making unverified assumptions.
- Include every terminal command executed, from the initial Nmap scan through privilege escalation checks (including the exact `cat` / `type` commands used to read flags).
- Include the complete terminal output: Nmap port results, OS versions, web service banners, software versions.
- For public exploits or custom PoCs, include the exploit code directly in the designated template section, highlighting custom modifications. Every interaction with the target host must be meticulously recorded.

---

## Flag Proofs & the Mandatory Host IP Screenshot Rule

You capture two distinct flag tiers:
1. **Initial Access** — the low-privilege user flag (`local.txt`).
2. **Administrative Access** — the elevated superuser flag (`proof.txt`).

> [!warning] Strict screenshot rule
> The screenshot showing the flag content **MUST also display the host's network IP address within the exact same terminal window.**

- **Linux targets:** `cat local.txt` or `cat proof.txt` alongside `ip a` or `ifconfig`.
- **Windows targets:** `type local.txt` or `type proof.txt` alongside `ipconfig`.

This ensures flags were retrieved directly from the assigned target host rather than staged through an external proxy. Many candidates also include `whoami` in the same prompt to provide explicit visual proof of administrative privileges.

---

## Documenting Exploit Code & Payload Generation

- **Modified exploit code:** If you modify a public exploit (IP, port, app path, credentials), paste the **full** code into your report, **explicitly highlight the modified lines**, and explain why those adjustments were necessary.
- **Source attribution:** Always include reference URLs linking to the public vulnerability/exploit repository (Exploit-DB, Searchsploit, GitHub).
- **Payload generation:** If your exploit needs standalone shellcode or a reverse shell (e.g. `msfvenom`), document the exact generation command, the payload architecture selected, the listening host/port parameters, and include screenshots of the generator's terminal output.

## Choosing the Right Template

Documenting into an unorganized blank document leads to messy formatting. Use a structured, professional template:

- You may draft in Markdown, Microsoft Word, or LibreOffice Writer if you have strong formatting skills.
- **Using the official OffSec Exam Report Template is strongly recommended.** OffSec provides it in Microsoft Word (`.docx`) and LibreOffice formats.
- The official template ensures compliance with grading requirements: pre-configured indices, standardized headings, and clear placeholders for command syntax, screenshots, exploit code, and remediation.

> [!tip] Reproducibility is an essential grading standard
> The examiner will attempt to replicate your exact exploitation chain step-by-step. If an evaluator follows your written instructions verbatim, the machine **must** be successfully compromised. Ensure your steps are clear and easy to follow.

## Note-Taking Strategies During the Exam

Before the technical 24h window closes, verify all screenshots, commands, and outputs are saved locally. Once the technical exam ends, **the VPN connection terminates permanently** — you cannot recover missing proof files.

Two common workflow approaches:
1. **Documenting progressively** — logging every command, note, and screenshot immediately as attacks are executed.
2. **Re-solving systems for clean capture** — (author's personal method) solve the machines first, then revert the target to a clean state and run the exploit chain a second time specifically to capture clean outputs, organized command sequences, and uncluttered screenshots.

Both are viable, but if you are short on time, taking notes progressively ensures you do not run out of time.

For note organization: the author stored terminal commands and raw outputs in **Sublime Text** before transferring to the main Windows workstation. **OneNote**, **Notion**, **CherryTree**, or **Obsidian** work equally well. Drafting a concise 10–15 point skeletal summary per machine helps you audit which screenshots are gathered and ensures nothing is omitted.

---

## Technical Assumptions vs. Explicit Reproducibility

The evaluator grading your report is a technically competent professional, but they do **not** know the exact path you took. Do not make unverified assumptions:

- If you generate a reverse shell executable, document the command used to construct the payload.
- If you obtain a Windows command prompt and stage an enumeration binary like `winPEAS.exe`, clearly document **how** it was transferred (e.g. `certutil`, PowerShell `Invoke-WebRequest`, or `curl`).
- You don't need to define what common utilities like `curl` are, but you must document the exact syntax used to execute the transfer.
- Ensure all terminal inputs and outputs are captured accurately.

---

## Polish & Proofreading

Reports should be **concise and direct**: enough technical depth for an evaluator to reproduce your steps, with no unnecessary filler.

- Don't write lengthy essays on the history of an exploit or tool; present the exploit code, your modifications, and the resulting shell.
- Eliminate grammatical errors, formatting inconsistencies, and spelling mistakes. Minor typos may not fail you, but unclear instructions that prevent reproduction **can** cost points.
- Once compiled, **proofread the entire document at least two to three times**. Verify every command, flag hash, screenshot, and IP address is accurately accounted for.
