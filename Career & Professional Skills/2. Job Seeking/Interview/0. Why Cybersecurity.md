# Formal (Longer)

## Why Cybersecurity?

For me, cybersecurity wasn't a career decision that I made in college—it has been a pattern throughout my entire life.

I got my first Windows PC when I was around four years old. Like most kids, I spent hours watching YouTube and playing games online. But I wasn't just interested in playing games—I became fascinated by the fact that people could actually change them.

I remember finding websites that hosted modified versions of games with unlimited money, unlocked assets, and features that normally weren't available. Then I started playing GTA San Andreas and discovered cheat codes that let you fly, remove the wanted level, spawn vehicles, and completely change how the game behaved.

Most children would simply enjoy using those cheats.

I kept asking myself one question:

**"How is this even possible?"**

Looking back, I think that was the first time I became interested in understanding software instead of simply using it.

A few years later, when I was in fourth grade, I got my first smartphone—a Samsung Galaxy Young.

Most kids would have installed social media or more games.

The very first thing I wanted to do was root the phone.

I spent hours watching YouTube tutorials, learning about Android, rooting tools, and device customization because I wanted to understand what root access actually meant and why it gave so much control over the operating system.

Eventually, I pushed things too far and completely bricked my brand-new phone. It wouldn't boot anymore, I lost all my data, and I had to take it to a Samsung service center to get it repaired.

At the time it felt like a disaster.

Today I look at it as one of the most valuable lessons I've ever learned.

It taught me that experimenting without understanding a system has consequences, and from that day onward I became much more interested in understanding _why_ something worked instead of simply following tutorials.

As Android games became more popular, I naturally became interested in how mobile applications actually worked internally. I explored Android applications, APK structures, permissions, and reverse engineering concepts because I wanted to understand how software could be modified and why those modifications were possible.

For a few years I focused mainly on school.

Then COVID happened.

During the lockdown I had a lot more time to learn, and my curiosity came back stronger than ever.

I was playing multiplayer games and, like many players, I kept encountering people using cheats. Instead of simply wondering where they came from, I became fascinated by the technology behind them.

That curiosity introduced me to reverse engineering concepts, scripting, networking, software internals, client-server communication, and how distributed applications actually function.

Without realizing it, I had stopped learning about games.

I had started learning computer systems.

As my understanding grew, I started building my own lab environments.

Very quickly I realized I needed cloud infrastructure.

Initially I rented inexpensive virtual servers because they were convenient, but after some time I thought,

_"Why don't I learn how to build and manage my own infrastructure instead?"_

That single question led me into Microsoft Azure.

I learned how to deploy virtual machines, configure networking, assign public IP addresses, secure cloud infrastructure, and manage both Windows and Linux environments.

Looking back, it's funny because I never woke up one morning and decided to learn cloud computing.

Security naturally pushed me there.

Around the same time I also became fascinated by hardware security.

I bought devices like the Flipper Zero, HackRF, Raspberry Pi, and Alfa Wi-Fi adapters because I wanted to understand how wireless communication actually worked beyond software.

I built projects involving RFID, NFC, software-defined radio, Wi-Fi, GPS spoofing concepts, wireless protocols, and embedded systems.

I explored how aviation GPS spoofing incidents occur, how cellular communication is structured, and why technologies like modern payment cards and metro cards are resistant to cloning through cryptographic protections and tokenization.

Those projects taught me that cybersecurity isn't just software.

It's hardware.

It's networking.

It's cloud.

It's operating systems.

It's distributed systems.

It's engineering.

I documented many of these projects on GitHub and LinkedIn because I genuinely enjoy building things and sharing what I learn.

By my third year of college I realized something important.

Nobody was forcing me to study cybersecurity.

Nobody was giving me assignments.

I was voluntarily spending my evenings, weekends, and vacations learning it simply because I enjoyed it.

That was the moment I decided to turn my passion into my profession.

I joined formal cybersecurity training and earned certifications like CEH and Security+.

During that time, one of my instructors gave me feedback that completely changed my perspective.

He told me,

_"You understand security, but your networking fundamentals aren't strong enough."_

Instead of treating that as criticism, I treated it as a roadmap.

I went back and studied networking properly.

I earned both CCNA and CompTIA Network+ because I realized that you can't secure systems if you don't fully understand how they communicate.

Later, I watched a technical talk about SS7 vulnerabilities in telecommunications.

I became so interested that I reached out directly to the founder of a cybersecurity startup on LinkedIn and simply asked if I could learn from his team.

That message eventually led to my first cybersecurity internship.

There I worked on vulnerability assessments, network monitoring, Ubuntu infrastructure, AWS environments, and enterprise security projects for real clients.

One experience from that internship completely changed the way I approach learning.

While deploying infrastructure in AWS, I accidentally left a large cloud instance running longer than intended, which generated unnecessary costs.

Instead of blaming the platform or treating it as bad luck, I asked myself why I had made that mistake.

The answer was simple.

I didn't understand cloud deeply enough.

That experience motivated me to study cloud architecture much more seriously, eventually earning both AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner and AWS Solutions Architect – Associate.

It transformed a mistake into one of the most valuable learning experiences of my career.

While working alongside government cyber cells during that internship, I was introduced to digital forensics and incident response, which led me to pursue CHFI and broaden my understanding beyond offensive security.

As I became more confident in my skills, I wanted to benchmark myself against the strongest candidates in the country.

Instead of applying only to local internships, I deliberately started applying to highly competitive national-level cybersecurity programs.

The first major milestone was being selected for the Indian Army's specialized cybersecurity internship after multiple technical interview rounds involving candidates from across India.

Working there exposed me to how secure military communication systems are designed and built, and knowing that some of the work our team contributed to would eventually support real operational environments gave me an entirely new appreciation for engineering secure systems.

Next, I was selected by CloudSEK, where I worked with security teams supporting Fortune 500 companies and major organizations such as Flipkart, Tata, Paytm, Medanta, and international clients.

One project that I still remember involved analyzing approximately 2,000 GitHub repositories for a BFSI client to identify potential payment-related secret leaks within a single day.

Almost everyone I asked initially said it wasn't realistically possible.

Instead of accepting that answer, I broke the problem into smaller pieces.

I wrote scripts to filter repositories, handled rate limiting, used automated secret-scanning tools, verified findings, filtered employee commits, removed duplicates, and gradually reduced roughly 2,000 repositories down to only two verified findings that were reported to the client.

That experience taught me one of the biggest lessons of my career.

When a problem looks impossible, don't try to solve everything at once.

Break it into smaller problems, solve each one methodically, and the larger problem becomes manageable.

That mindset has stayed with me ever since.

I also participated in our internal hackathon, where I proposed and built a security playbook to automate parts of the investigation workflow.

After experiencing military cybersecurity and enterprise product security, I wanted to understand cybersecurity from the public-sector perspective.

So I applied for the Gurugram Police Cyber Security Internship.

It brought together professionals from law enforcement, the Ministry of Home Affairs, the military, corporate security teams, and students from top universities.

It gave me a completely different perspective on how governments investigate cybercrime, coordinate across organizations, and respond to real-world incidents.

I finished **3rd overall among more than 15,000 applicants from across India.**

Looking back, something became very clear to me.

Every opportunity became bigger than the previous one.

From personal curiosity...

to formal learning...

to internships...

to national-level selection processes...

to working with the military...

enterprise security...

and law enforcement.

Each experience gave me a completely different perspective on cybersecurity.

Today, what excites me isn't simply understanding how systems can be broken.

What excites me is building systems that are secure from the beginning.

I enjoy understanding attackers because it helps me become a better defender.

I enjoy solving engineering problems.

I enjoy learning technologies that are completely new to me.

And I enjoy working on problems that have real impact.

When I look back now, I don't think there was ever one moment where I chose cybersecurity.

Every stage of my life naturally pushed me deeper into it.

Games led me to Android.

Android led me to reverse engineering.

Reverse engineering led me to networking.

Networking led me to cloud.

Cloud led me to infrastructure.

Infrastructure led me to hardware.

Hardware led me to security engineering.

Security engineering led me to opportunities with the Indian Army, CloudSEK, and Gurugram Police.

Every step built on the previous one.

That's why I believe Security Engineering is exactly where I belong.

And that's also why I'm excited about Google.

Google operates at a scale that very few organizations in the world do.

The opportunity to solve complex engineering problems that protect billions of users, while learning from some of the best security engineers in the industry, is exactly the environment where I believe I can contribute, continue learning, and have the greatest impact.
