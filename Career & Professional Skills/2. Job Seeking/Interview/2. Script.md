# Why Cybersecurity? (formal for interview)

```
For me, cybersecurity wasn't a career that I discovered during college—it has been a journey that started almost as soon as I started using computers.

I got my first Windows PC when I was around four years old. Like most kids, I spent a lot of time watching YouTube and playing games online. But what fascinated me wasn't just playing the games—it was discovering that people could actually change them.

I remember finding websites that hosted modified versions of games with unlimited money, unlimited resources, and unlocked features. Then I started playing games like GTA San Andreas and discovered cheat codes that let you fly, remove the wanted level, spawn vehicles, and completely change how the game behaved.

At that age, I obviously didn't understand programming or security, but I remember constantly wondering, _"How is this possible? How can someone change software that millions of people are using?"_

Looking back, I think that curiosity was my very first introduction to cybersecurity, even though I didn't know what cybersecurity was.

A few years later, when I was in fourth grade, I got my first smartphone—a Samsung Galaxy Young.

Most children would have installed social media or games. The very first thing I wanted to do was root the phone because I had learned that root access gave complete control over the device.

I spent hours watching YouTube tutorials, experimenting with Android customization, rooting tools like KingRoot, and applications like Lucky Patcher because I wanted to understand what additional privileges actually meant.

Eventually, I pushed things a little too far and completely bricked my brand-new phone. It wouldn't boot anymore, all my data was gone, and I had to take it to a Samsung service center to get it repaired.

Although it felt terrible at the time, it taught me one of my earliest engineering lessons: if you don't truly understand a system, experimenting blindly can break it. From that point onward, I became much more interested in understanding _why_ something worked instead of simply following tutorials.

As Android games became more popular, I became interested in how mobile applications worked internally. I explored APK modification, application patching, Android permissions, and reverse engineering concepts. Again, what interested me wasn't simply modifying an application—it was understanding how those modifications were possible.

For a few years, I focused mostly on school.

Then during the COVID lockdown, my curiosity came back stronger than ever.

I was playing PUBG Mobile, and like many players I encountered people using cheats. Instead of simply wondering where those cheats came from, I became fascinated by the technology behind them.

I started reading about game security, reverse engineering, memory modification concepts, scripting, APK analysis, networking, and client-server communication. I spent countless hours reading technical forums, watching talks, learning from online communities, and trying to understand how complex software systems behaved under the hood.

What started as curiosity about games gradually expanded into curiosity about operating systems, networking, software architecture, and distributed systems.

As I built more of my own lab environments, I realized I needed cloud infrastructure.

Initially, I rented small virtual servers because they were inexpensive. But after some time, I thought, _"Why am I renting servers from someone else? Why don't I learn how to build and manage my own cloud infrastructure?"_

That question led me to Microsoft Azure.

I learned how to create virtual machines, configure networking, assign public IP addresses, manage firewalls, and deploy Windows and Linux instances. Without realizing it, my curiosity about security had naturally led me into cloud computing.

Around the same time, I also became fascinated by hardware security.

I bought devices like the Flipper Zero, HackRF, Raspberry Pi, and Alfa Wi-Fi adapters because I wanted to understand wireless communication beyond software.

I started building projects involving RFID, NFC, software-defined radio, Wi-Fi, GPS spoofing concepts, and wireless protocols. I explored how aviation GPS spoofing works, how cellular communication is structured, and why modern payment cards and metro cards resist cloning through cryptographic protections and tokenization.

These projects taught me that security isn't just about software—it spans hardware, networking, cloud infrastructure, embedded systems, and wireless communications.

I documented many of these projects on GitHub and LinkedIn because I genuinely enjoy building things and sharing what I learn.

By my third year of college, I realized something important.

Nobody was asking me to spend evenings and weekends studying cybersecurity.

I was doing it because I genuinely enjoyed it.

That was the point where I decided to turn my passion into my profession.

I enrolled in formal cybersecurity training and earned certifications such as CEH and Security+.

During that process, one of my instructors told me something that completely changed my perspective.

He said,

_"You understand security, but your networking fundamentals aren't strong enough."_

Instead of taking that as criticism, I treated it as a roadmap.

I went back, studied networking properly, earned CCNA and CompTIA Network+, and developed a much stronger understanding of how modern systems communicate because I realized that you can't secure something you don't fully understand.

Later, I watched a technical presentation about SS7 vulnerabilities in telecommunications.

I became so interested that I reached out directly to the founder of a cybersecurity startup on LinkedIn and asked if I could learn from his team.

That message eventually led to my first cybersecurity internship.

There, I worked on vulnerability assessments, network monitoring, enterprise infrastructure, Ubuntu servers, AWS environments, and security tooling for real clients.

During that internship, I made another mistake that became a valuable lesson.

While working with AWS, I accidentally left a large cloud instance running longer than intended, which generated unnecessary costs.

That experience reinforced the importance of operational discipline and cloud governance.

Rather than treating it as a failure, I decided to strengthen my cloud knowledge even further and earned both AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner and AWS Solutions Architect – Associate certifications.

While working alongside government cyber cells during that internship, I was introduced to digital forensics and incident response.

That sparked another area of interest, which eventually led me to pursue CHFI and broaden my understanding of cybersecurity beyond offensive security.

Throughout college, I deliberately explored many different branches of computer science.

I explored software engineering.

I explored cloud computing.

I explored DevOps.

I explored AI.

After spending time in each of those domains, I realized that cybersecurity was the one field that consistently excited me the most.

It combines operating systems, networking, cloud infrastructure, software engineering, hardware, and continuous problem-solving in a way that no other discipline does.

As I became more confident in my skills, I wanted to benchmark myself against the best candidates in the country.

Instead of limiting myself to local opportunities, I started applying for highly competitive national-level cybersecurity internships and programs.

The first major milestone was being selected for the Indian Army's specialized cybersecurity internship program.

The selection process involved multiple technical interviews with thousands of applicants from across India competing for a very limited number of positions.

Being selected into such a specialized technical program gave me confidence that my skills were beginning to stand out on a national level.

Next, I participated in another nationwide selection process with CloudSEK, one of India's leading product-based cybersecurity companies.

Once again, I competed against applicants from across the country, cleared the interview rounds, and earned my place.

That showed me that my skills could translate not only to government environments but also to high-growth product companies solving real-world security problems.

At that point, I had experienced military and private-sector cybersecurity.

I wanted to understand the law enforcement side as well.

So I applied for the Gurugram Police Cyber Security Internship.

It was another highly competitive national-level selection process with applicants from across India.

After being selected, I went on to finish **3rd overall among more than 15,000 applicants nationwide**.

Looking back, that progression became a defining moment for me.

Three consecutive national-level selection processes.

Three completely different environments—military, product security, and law enforcement.

And being able to earn opportunities in each of them gave me confidence that I wasn't just passionate about cybersecurity—I could also perform in highly competitive environments.

That was the point where I realized there was no looking back.

Today, what motivates me isn't simply understanding how systems fail.

What excites me is building systems that are secure, resilient, and trustworthy.

I enjoy thinking like an attacker because it helps me become a better defender.

I enjoy learning because cybersecurity evolves every single day.

And I enjoy solving engineering problems that protect people at scale.

That's exactly why Security Engineering appeals to me.

It's the perfect combination of software engineering, cloud infrastructure, distributed systems, operating systems, networking, and security.

And that's also why I'm excited about Google.

Google operates at a scale that very few companies in the world do. The opportunity to work on security problems that protect billions of users, while continuously learning from some of the best security engineers in the industry, is exactly the kind of environment where I believe I can grow the most and contribute meaningful impact.

For me, cybersecurity isn't simply the profession I chose.

It's been a journey of curiosity that started with a four-year-old asking, _"How does this work?"_

That same question still motivates me today.
```
