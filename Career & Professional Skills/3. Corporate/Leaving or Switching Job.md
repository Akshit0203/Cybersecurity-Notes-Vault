## Resignation — The Right Way

### Never Discuss Resignation with Your Manager Before Resigning

Before resignation, should you be discussing about your resignation with your manager or not? Many people believe that having a formal discussion with your manager before putting your resignation in the system is ideal. But I never believe in it. I have never done that, and I will give you very strong reasons why:

1. **You already made up your mind** — There were reasons you decided to quit. There is no point going back to your manager at this point.
2. **It delays your new opportunity** — If you discuss it, your joining date at the new company may get delayed while you buy time.
3. **Your manager will try to persuade you** — They may try to influence your mind, leaving you in a confused state, which is not right at that point.
4. **Even after discussion, they won't be happy** — If you still decide to resign after the discussion, your manager is not going to be happy with you anyway.

**What to do instead:** Resign in the system first, then block your manager's time, and have a one-to-one discussion with them afterwards. That has always worked and makes the journey smooth.

### Once You Resign — Never Go Back

> My one and only rule: Once you resign, never go back even if they match the offer. Your life will be easier.

### Never Withdraw Your Resignation on Verbal Promises

A real incident: Varun was a key performer — six years in a global organization. He got an offer with a better role and better money. He resigned, and his manager was worried because he was a key team player. HR stepped in and said, "Don't worry, withdraw your resignation. We'll match the offer. Trust us."

Varun shared a good relationship with his manager and HR, so based on their assurance, he withdrew his resignation. One week passed, 10 days, 14 days — same response: "The approval is still pending, don't worry, we'll figure it out."

Meanwhile, Varun got deeply occupied with a go-live project — critical deadlines, high pressure. After four weeks, he followed up with HR again, and that's when they said: "We can't match it. We're sorry. Best we can do is 10%."

By then, the new offer had expired. His leverage was gone. He had no other option left.

**The real problem:** Everything was verbal. No email confirmation at all.

> **Rule:** Never withdraw your resignation unless a revised offer is officially released or there is an email confirmation from HR. If it's not in an email, it did not happen.

### Resignation Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Always send a formal email** — Don't just tell your manager verbally. Keep a screenshot or PDF of your resignation email and the acceptance email. You will need it when applying for new jobs.
2. **Check the notice period clause** — Does your company allow buyouts? Does it allow leave adjustments against notice period? Do they want you to serve the entire notice period, or do they let you leave early?
3. **Never assume things** — Always know:
    - When your last day will be
    - When you'll get your full and final settlement
    - When you'll receive your relieving letter and experience letter
    - How background verification will be done after you leave
4. **Always try to leave on a positive note** — Exit smartly, not in a hurry.

## Is It Okay to Leave After 6 Months or 1 Year?

This is a really tricky situation that many people face in their first job. Maybe the work environment is toxic, or you're not learning anything, or you got a much better offer, or the company culture is just not what you expected. But you're worried — will leaving so early look bad on my resume? Will future employers reject me for job hopping?

**The honest truth:** Leaving a job after 6 months to 1 year is not ideal, but sometimes it's necessary and justified.

**When to consider leaving:**
- Toxic workplace
- No growth
- Mental health suffering
- Significantly better opportunity

> Your career and well-being matter more than what looks good on paper.

**When to stick it out:** If it's just minor inconveniences or you're bored, try to complete at least 1–2 years.

**If you do leave early:**
- Be honest about why you left during interviews
- Focus on positive reasons like seeking growth, better opportunities, or skill development
- One early exit won't ruin your career, but don't make it a pattern
- Choose your next company more carefully and try to stay longer there

## Essential Documents to Collect Before Leaving a Job

To avoid major HR or salary issues later, collect the following documents before switching jobs:

| # | Document | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Experience Letter** | Official proof of employment; without it, background verification or your start date at a new job may be delayed. |
| 2 | **Relieving Letter** | Confirms you left on good terms; required for onboarding at some companies. No relieving letter = red flag for future employers. |
| 3 | **Last 3 Months' Salary Slips** | Necessary for background checks and salary negotiations; keep both soft and hard copies. No proof = weak negotiation power. |
| 4 | **Form 16 / Tax Documents** | Essential for tax filing and switching payrolls. Chasing HR later for this is a nightmare. |
| 5 | **PF & UAN Details** | Required for transferring or withdrawing provident funds. |
| 6 | **Offer Letter + Promotion/Increment Letters** | Useful for proving career growth, verification, or loan applications. Helps justify your growth and negotiate better. |
| 7 | **Clearance Letter (if applicable)** | Proves you have returned company assets and cleared all dues. |
