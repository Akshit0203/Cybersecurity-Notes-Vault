## Hard Work vs. Showing Hard Work

**It doesn't matter whether you are actually doing hard work or not, but showing that you are doing hard work matters the most.**

There used to be a guy in our office who would arrive at 1:00 PM and would often disappear for two hours during lunch. However, when it was time to leave the office — especially when the boss was leaving — he would be at his desk talking loudly about work. Even if the official exit time was 6:00 PM, he would go home and send compiled work to the boss at 8:00 PM. He created an image that he was always working both before and after office hours.

Even if he wasn't active in the group chat all week, he would definitely be active on Sunday to show that he works even on his day off. He projected the image of being that one employee who is **always working, always answering, and taking ownership**.

The lesson is that doing hard work is not as necessary as **showing that you are doing hard work**. You should **shout about your work from the rooftops**; if you arrive early or stay late, post a social media story about how much you "love your work". Doing this will ensure you get your appraisal quickly.

## Dealing with Bad Managers

### Signs Your Manager Is the Problem — Not You

1. **Everything is urgent, all the time** — If your boss creates fake urgency daily, it's not your fault. It's poor management.
2. **Feedback only when you're wrong** — If you only hear from them when you mess up, you're not bad at your job. They just don't know how to lead.
3. **Constantly changing expectations** — If targets keep changing, you're not confused… they are.
4. **They take credit, you take blame** — If your work gets no recognition but mistakes get highlighted, that's not a performance issue… that's toxic leadership.
5. **You feel anxious before talking to them** — If you feel fear instead of comfort, it's not "professional pressure"… it's a bad boss.

> If your manager is doing this… **YOU** are not the problem. Your manager is.

### What to Do

- Don't report managers
- Don't say anything
- At max, change teams

## Background Verification (BGV) — How to Protect Yourself

Background checks feel scary when your exit wasn't clean.
But remember: BGV is a compliance process — not a revenge tool.

### 1. Understand What's Actually Verified

Most companies verify:

- Employment dates
- Designation
- Reporting manager
- Exit type (voluntary/involuntary)

They usually don't share detailed performance gossip.
Fear grows in imagination.

### 2. Control Your Narrative Early

If asked about your exit, keep it neutral:

> "The role expectations evolved, and it wasn't a long-term alignment. I've since strengthened X and Y."

No blaming. No emotional storytelling.
Confidence reduces suspicion.

### 3. Prepare Alternative References

If your direct manager was toxic:

- Use previous managers
- Cross-functional leads
- Senior colleagues
- Clients

Strong references balance risk.

### 4. Keep Documentation

Save:

- Offer letter
- Relieving letter
- Salary slips
- Appraisals
- Appreciation emails

Facts > feelings.

### 5. Stay Consistent Across Platforms

Your resume, LinkedIn, and interview story must align.
Inconsistency triggers red flags faster than a tough exit.

### 6. Don't Lie

Short-term cover-ups destroy long-term credibility.
Companies reject dishonesty more than performance gaps.

### Final Truth

A toxic manager cannot destroy your career unless you panic and lose composure.

Clarity + documentation + calm framing makes BGV manageable.

Your next employer cares more about who you are now than who disliked you before.

## 10 Signs You're Becoming Irrelevant at Work

> Signs you're no longer relevant in your team / potential layoff warning.

1. You're not included in important meetings anymore
2. Your opinion is rarely asked
3. Decisions are made without looping you in
4. Your work is repetitive — no growth tasks
5. Someone junior is leading what you used to handle
6. You're the last to know about team updates
7. Your manager gives minimal feedback
8. No one depends on you for critical work
9. You're not part of new initiatives/projects
10. You feel "present" but not "needed"

This doesn't mean you're not capable. It means you need to reposition yourself.

**Start by:**

- Taking ownership
- Speaking up in meetings
- Upskilling (especially in AI/tools)
- Making your work visible

> _Relevance is not permanent. It's created daily._

## Hard Truths About the Corporate Ladder

> There's nothing at the end of the corporate ladder.

1. Your imposter syndrome will be cured once you see how higher management makes decisions.
2. Skillsets and degrees get you the job; relationship & visibility help you stay in the job.
3. Don't let anyone tell you confidence doesn't matter — it is usually the last (or only) factor for a decision.
4. The 1-month notice is for you to document down all your YEARS of expertise + hard-work into a SOP so that the next new hire who has more energy, takes half your salary, can copy you.
5. You're just a number in an excel sheet.

## Mistakes I Made in Corporate

1. **I assumed my hard work would speak for itself** — In reality, you need to make your work and achievements visible by shamelessly speaking about them.
2. **As a perfectionist, I avoided opportunities for feedback before my work was complete.**
3. **I constantly worried about what my colleagues thought of me** — It distracted me from my actual work, and to be honest, who cares? We're all here to deliver at the end of the day.
4. **Initially, I didn't speak about my work like it mattered** — I would trail off or say things like, "this isn't important so I'll keep it short" about entire projects. All this did was lose people's faith in my work — if you speak about things like they're important, people will believe so.
5. **I thought working more = working harder** — Now I'm all about delivering high-quality outcomes, and that doesn't necessarily correlate with late hours and a packed schedule. Efficiency is not a crime!

## Career-Killing Mistakes That Silently Block Promotion

### #1 — I Confused Hard Work With High Impact

I stayed late, took on extra tasks, and was always "busy," but none of that mattered. What mattered was whether my work **moved a metric, solved a visible problem, or reduced someone else's pain**. Most of what I did was effort-heavy and impact-light.

- **Why it blocked my promotion:** Promotions reward leverage, not labor; my output was real but replaceable.
- **What I fixed:** I started asking, "If I do this well, who benefits — and how will they notice?" If there was no clear answer, I deprioritized or reframed the work.

### #2 — I Stayed Quiet Waiting for the "Right Moment"

I believed speaking up required a brilliant insight, so I waited. Meetings ended and decisions were made while louder, less-prepared people shaped the narrative and I stayed invisible.

- **Why it blocked my promotion:** Leadership is associated with **presence**; silence looked like disengagement rather than thoughtfulness.
- **What I fixed:** I forced myself to **speak within the first 5 minutes** of every meeting, even if only for clarification. Once my voice existed in the room, contributing later became easy.

### #3 — I Trusted My Manager to Remember My Wins

I assumed my manager noticed everything I delivered, but they didn't because they were managing chaos across multiple teams.

- **Why it blocked my promotion:** When promotion discussions happened, my contributions weren't top-of-mind.
- **What I fixed:** I started maintaining a **weekly brag sheet** of outcomes delivered, problems solved, and metrics improved. I shared these factual highlights during 1:1s and reviews because advocacy needs data.

### #4 — I Avoided Conflict to Stay "Nice"

I let bad decisions slide, didn't challenge timelines, and nodded when I disagreed. I thought I was being mature, but I was actually being forgettable.

- **Why it blocked my promotion:** Leaders are expected to have a point of view; I looked agreeable, not dependable.
- **What I fixed:** I learned to **disagree calmly and privately** by pointing out underestimated risks. Respectful pushback built trust, whereas passive agreement destroyed it.

### #5 — I Waited to Be "Chosen" Instead of Acting Promotable

This was the biggest mistake. I believed promotions were rewards received after doing enough good work, but they are actually decisions made when someone is **already operating at the next level**.

- **Why it blocked my promotion:** I waited for permission instead of behaving like the role I wanted.
- **What I fixed:** I started owning outcomes instead of just tasks, closing loops without reminders, and **thinking like my manager** rather than just following my job description. The title followed the behavior.

### Final Reality Check

> If your promotion keeps getting delayed, stop asking "What else should I do?" and start asking **"What signals am I sending — intentionally or not?"**
>
> Careers don't stall because of incompetence; they stall because of **invisibility, misinterpretation, and unspoken assumptions**. Fix the signals, and the outcomes change.
