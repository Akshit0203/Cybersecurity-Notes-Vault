Describe how your work, research, educational and other professional experiences have prepared you for graduate studies at the INI and how you stand out among your peers?

Throughout my undergraduate education, I deliberately pursued experiences that exposed me to different dimensions of cybersecurity instead of specializing too early. Together, these experiences strengthened both my technical foundation and my appreciation for how cybersecurity intersects with engineering, operations, and public safety.

What distinguishes me is not simply the number of internships I have completed, but the intention behind them. Each experience filled a different gap in my understanding and reinforced my goal of becoming a security engineer capable of building resilient systems. I believe Carnegie Mellon's Information Networking Institute is the ideal environment to integrate these experiences into a deeper understanding of secure systems, collaborate across disciplines, and develop solutions to increasingly complex security challenges.

## Essay 3

From your experience as a student/employee working on a team, describe a time when you struggled. How did you resolve the challenges and contribute to the team? Be specific drawing from your experience as a student or as an employee. If you are using a student example, please use a course or project from your transcript. If you are using an employee example, please use a job or project on your resume.

----

During my internship at CloudSEK, I was assigned a time-sensitive investigation for a BFSI client involving the analysis of GitHub repositories and historical commits to identify potential payment-related information leaks. I had only one day to complete the investigation, despite having no prior experience performing this type of large-scale repository analysis. Initially, the scope of the task felt overwhelming, and I realized I could not solve it alone.

My first instinct was to ask experienced teammates how they would approach the investigation. Most believed the task could not realistically be completed within the available time because of its complexity. Instead of treating that as the end of the discussion, I changed my approach. Rather than asking one person for the entire solution, I sought guidance from different teammates on individual components of the problem. One explained repository enumeration, another suggested techniques for commit history analysis, while others shared practical approaches for identifying exposed sensitive information. By combining these independent pieces of knowledge, I designed a structured workflow that allowed me to complete the investigation within the deadline.

More importantly, the experience changed how I think about teamwork. I learned that effective collaboration is not about finding one person with every answer, but about leveraging the diverse expertise of a team and integrating those perspectives into a coherent solution. It also reinforced the importance of remaining adaptable when initial approaches fail.

Since then, I have approached technical challenges by breaking complex problems into manageable components, communicating openly with teammates, and treating collaboration as a force multiplier rather than a last resort. I believe this mindset will allow me to contribute effectively to the collaborative and interdisciplinary environment at Carnegie Mellon's Information Networking Institute.
