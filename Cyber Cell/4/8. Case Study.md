# Case Study: DDoS Attack Investigation Through Malware-Infected Restaurant QR Codes

## Initial Incident

A startup company was experiencing a large-scale DDoS attack.

The attack traffic appeared to originate from hundreds of normal users.

At first glance:

- No obvious botnet infrastructure was visible.
    
- Traffic came from legitimate residential IP addresses.
    
- Attackers appeared to be ordinary citizens.
    

The key question was:

> Why are so many normal people attacking a startup?

---

## Phase 1 – Traffic Analysis

Investigators collected:

- Network logs
    
- Firewall logs
    
- ISP records
    
- Traffic records covering approximately two weeks
    

Analysis showed:

- Thousands of requests originating from consumer internet connections.
    
- Traffic distributed across many users.
    
- Behavior consistent with a botnet.
    

---

## Phase 2 – Victim Attribution

Through legal processes and telecom cooperation, investigators identified subscribers associated with the attacking IP addresses.

Interviews revealed:

- Most users were ordinary citizens.
    
- None knew the startup.
    
- None had ever visited the startup's website intentionally.
    

This suggested:

> The users were victims, not attackers.

---

## Phase 3 – Device Examination

Several affected users voluntarily provided devices for analysis.

Investigators discovered malware infections.

The malware was responsible for:

- Generating automated traffic
    
- Participating in the DDoS attack
    
- Operating without user knowledge
    

The devices had become part of a botnet.

---

## Phase 4 – Malware Analysis

Reverse engineering revealed:

- Remote command-and-control capability
    
- Automated traffic generation
    
- Persistence mechanisms
    

The malware family was known in underground communities and had similarities to malware previously associated with Russian-speaking cybercriminal ecosystems.

However:

- Malware origin does not automatically identify the attacker.
    
- Criminals often purchase malware from underground forums.
    

This raised a new question:

> How were so many unrelated victims infected?

---

## Phase 5 – Common Factor Analysis

Investigators compared:

- Locations
    
- Device types
    
- Browsing history
    
- Recent activities
    

A surprising pattern emerged.

Many victims had recently visited the same restaurant in Connaught Place.

---

## Phase 6 – Restaurant Investigation

Restaurant staff were interviewed.

No unusual activity was initially reported.

However investigators discovered:

- Physical menus had been replaced.
    
- Customers were instructed to scan a QR code.
    
- The QR code opened a digital menu.
    

This became a major lead.

---

## Phase 7 – QR Code Analysis

Investigation revealed:

- The QR code redirected users through a compromised server.
    
- Under specific conditions, visitors received malware.
    
- Infection occurred silently.
    

As a result:

- Customers believed they were simply viewing a menu.
    
- Their devices became infected.
    
- The malware later participated in DDoS operations.
    

---

## Phase 8 – Why Target a Startup?

This remained the central mystery.

Questions included:

- Why infect random restaurant visitors?
    
- Why target a startup?
    
- Why deploy such a complex attack?
    

The answer was not yet apparent.

---

## Phase 9 – Who Compromised the Restaurant?

Investigators analyzed:

- Restaurant systems
    
- Access logs
    
- Administrative records
    
- CCTV footage
    

A behavioral anomaly appeared.

One individual repeatedly visited the restaurant.

Characteristics:

- Arrived almost daily
    
- Ordered the same meal
    
- Remained for 2–5 hours
    
- Continued visiting before and after the attack period
    

The behavior was unusual enough to warrant investigation.

---

## Phase 10 – Identification of the Suspect

The individual was located and interviewed.

Initially he appeared harmless.

Background checks revealed:

- Former intern
    
- Previously worked for a company connected to the startup ecosystem
    
- Had a grievance regarding how he was treated
    

The motive appeared to be revenge.

---

## Phase 11 – Underground Malware Acquisition

Digital evidence revealed:

- The suspect searched cybercrime forums.
    
- Attempted to purchase malware.
    
- Found the price beyond his budget.
    

Instead of paying cash:

- He performed small hacking tasks for criminals.
    
- Exchanged services in a barter arrangement.
    
- Eventually gained access to malware tools.
    

---

## Phase 12 – Compromise of the Restaurant Infrastructure

Using acquired tools and knowledge:

- The suspect compromised restaurant systems.
    
- Modified QR-code-related infrastructure.
    
- Distributed malware through the digital menu process.
    

The restaurant itself was not the final target.

It was simply the infection vector.

---

## Phase 13 – Building the Botnet

Over time:

1. Customers visited the restaurant.
    
2. QR codes infected devices.
    
3. Devices joined the botnet.
    
4. The attacker waited.
    
5. The botnet grew quietly.
    

No attack occurred immediately.

The infrastructure was planted and allowed to mature.

---

## Phase 14 – Launching the DDoS Attack

Once enough devices were infected:

- Commands were issued.
    
- Botnet traffic activated.
    
- Requests were directed at the startup.
    

The startup experienced a large-scale DDoS event.

---

## Key Investigation Lessons

### 1. Attack Traffic Does Not Always Identify the Attacker

The source IPs belonged to victims, not criminals.

---

### 2. Botnets Often Use Innocent Users

Ordinary citizens may unknowingly participate in attacks through malware infections.

---

### 3. Common-Factor Analysis Is Powerful

A single shared restaurant visit connected hundreds of unrelated victims.

---

### 4. Physical and Digital Investigations Must Be Combined

The breakthrough came from:

- Telecom data
    
- Malware analysis
    
- CCTV footage
    
- Human interviews
    
- Restaurant records
    

---

### 5. Sophisticated Attacks Are Often Prepared Long Before Execution

Attackers frequently:

1. Compromise infrastructure.
    
2. Establish persistence.
    
3. Build victim populations.
    
4. Wait.
    
5. Launch the operation later.
    

The malware may be planted weeks or months before the actual attack occurs.

---

### 6. Malware Is Often Planted Well in Advance

One important lesson is that attackers rarely infect devices and launch an attack on the same day.

Instead, they typically:

1. Plant malware quietly.
    
2. Allow infections to spread over time.
    
3. Build a sufficiently large botnet.
    
4. Remain dormant to avoid detection.
    
5. Activate the malware only when the desired target is chosen.
    

This delay between infection and attack can range from days to months. As a result, investigators must look far back in time when tracing the origin of an incident, because the initial compromise often occurs long before the visible attack begins.

---

## Key Takeaway

Modern cyber investigations often begin with technical evidence but are solved by connecting digital artifacts, human behavior, physical locations, timelines, and motive. The most important clue in this case was not the malware itself—it was the discovery that hundreds of unrelated victims shared a single real-world location.

Another critical takeaway is that cyberattacks are often staged well in advance. Malware is frequently planted long before it is used, allowing attackers to quietly build infrastructure, gather victims, and prepare for an operation. By the time the attack becomes visible, the real compromise may have occurred weeks or even months earlier.
