# Can Governments Access a Phone by Sending a Link?

## Common Claim

A common claim is:

> "Governments can compromise a phone through a malicious link and gain access to photos, messages, files, and other data."

This claim is not entirely unfounded. Advanced government agencies, intelligence organizations, and specialized cyber units are known to acquire, develop, or purchase sophisticated exploits that can compromise devices under certain conditions.

In some cases, a malicious link may be enough to trigger an exploit. If a vulnerability exists, clicking the link could potentially allow an attacker to gain access to device functions such as files, messages, location data, and even hardware components like the camera or microphone without requesting normal user permissions.

There have also been reports of exploits that can be triggered through specially crafted calls or communication requests. In certain cases, a missed call, message, or other interaction may be enough to exploit a vulnerability if one exists on the target device. The key factor is not the link, call, or message itself, but the underlying software vulnerability being exploited.

---

## What Is a Zero-Click or One-Click Exploit?

Advanced spyware may exploit vulnerabilities in:

- Messaging applications
    
- Browsers
    
- Operating systems
    
- Media processing components
    
- Calling and communication services
    

### One-Click Exploit

Requires:

- User clicking a malicious link
    

The vulnerability is then exploited to execute code on the device.

In advanced cases, the exploit may gain privileges that allow access to sensitive device functions without displaying normal permission prompts.

### Zero-Click Exploit

Requires:

- No user interaction
    

The exploit may be triggered through specially crafted messages, calls, or data sent to the target device.

In some publicly reported cases, vulnerabilities in calling applications allowed attackers to target devices through incoming calls, even if the call was not answered.

---

## Government Access to Zero-Day Vulnerabilities

A zero-day vulnerability is a software flaw that is unknown to the vendor or has not yet been patched.

Many governments and intelligence agencies are reported to:

- Discover zero-day vulnerabilities
    
- Purchase exploits from specialized vendors
    
- Use advanced spyware platforms
    
- Conduct targeted surveillance operations
    

Because zero-days are unknown to the software developer at the time of use, they can sometimes bypass normal security protections until a patch is released.

The effectiveness of these operations depends on identifying and exploiting vulnerabilities in software. Without a vulnerability, simply sending a link, message, or call would not normally provide access to a device.

## Commercial Spyware and Mercenary Surveillance Vendors

Several advanced spyware platforms have been reported publicly.

Capabilities may include:

- Reading messages
    
- Accessing contacts
    
- Accessing photos
    
- Activating microphones
    
- Accessing cameras
    
- Tracking location
    

Such tools typically rely on previously unknown software vulnerabilities rather than ordinary permissions.

A number of these tools are developed by private companies often referred to as **mercenary spyware vendors**. These companies develop surveillance technologies and sell them to government customers, law enforcement agencies, or intelligence organizations.

---

## Pegasus: A Well-Known Example

One of the most widely reported spyware platforms is **Pegasus**, developed by the Israeli company NSO Group.

Public reports have alleged that Pegasus has been used against selected targets in multiple countries.

Reported capabilities have included:

- Accessing messages
    
- Reading emails
    
- Collecting location data
    
- Activating microphones
    
- Accessing cameras
    
- Extracting files
    
- Accessing photos stored on the device
    

Pegasus became notable because some versions reportedly used sophisticated zero-click exploits, allowing compromise without requiring the target to click a link.

Some publicly reported attack chains allegedly involved vulnerabilities in messaging or calling services, demonstrating that exploitation can sometimes occur through a message or even a missed call when a vulnerable component processes the incoming data.

It is important to note that Pegasus was designed for targeted surveillance and not for mass compromise of all devices.

Most investigations do not involve advanced spyware.

However, intelligence agencies and specialized national-security organizations may possess or acquire advanced capabilities for high-priority investigations involving terrorism, espionage, organized crime, or national security threats.

## Are There Government Backdoors in Every Phone?

There is no public evidence that every smartphone contains a universal government backdoor that allows unrestricted access at any time.

However, governments may gain access to specific devices by exploiting vulnerabilities, including zero-days, rather than relying on a built-in backdoor.

Modern smartphones use:

- Encryption
    
- Secure Enclave
    
- Trusted Execution Environments
    
- Sandboxing
    

These protections make compromise more difficult, but not always impossible when advanced exploits are available.

## Why Clicking a Link Can Be Dangerous

If a vulnerability exists:

User Clicks Link

↓

Malicious Code Executes

↓

Spyware Installed

↓

Data Accessed

In some situations, a successful exploit could potentially gain access to device resources such as:

- Files
    
- Messages
    
- Contacts
    
- Photos
    
- Camera
    
- Microphone
    
- Location data
    

without displaying the normal permission requests expected by users.

In some cases, highly sophisticated exploits may not even require the user to click anything. A vulnerable device may process a specially crafted message, media file, or call request automatically.

For example:

Missed Call or Message Received

↓

Vulnerable Component Processes Data

↓

Exploit Triggered

↓

Spyware Installed

↓

Photos, Messages, and Other Data Accessed

This is only possible when a vulnerability exists that allows the attacker to exploit the device.

The success of an attack depends on:

- Device version
    
- Security patches
    
- Exploit availability
    
- Target configuration
    
- User privileges
    
- Presence of an exploitable vulnerability

## Apple Lockdown Mode

To defend against highly sophisticated attacks, Apple introduced **Lockdown Mode**.

Lockdown Mode is designed specifically for individuals who may be targeted by advanced spyware, including:

- Journalists
    
- Activists
    
- Government officials
    
- Executives
    
- High-risk individuals
    

When enabled, Lockdown Mode significantly reduces the attack surface of the device by restricting certain features that have historically been targeted by advanced exploits.

Benefits include:

- Reduced exposure to zero-click attacks
    
- Additional protection against sophisticated spyware
    
- Hardening of messaging and web browsing components
    

As of current public reporting, Lockdown Mode has proven highly effective against many advanced spyware techniques, including methods previously associated with Pegasus. Security researchers have noted that Lockdown Mode substantially increases the difficulty of successfully compromising an iPhone using known Pegasus-style attack chains.

No security feature can guarantee absolute protection forever, but Lockdown Mode currently represents one of the strongest publicly available defenses against advanced mercenary spyware.

## Investigation Perspective

Modern investigations often rely on:

- Cloud records
    
- CDR/IPDR
    
- Device forensics
    
- Account logs
    
- Search warrants
    
- Platform cooperation
    

However, in high-value or targeted operations, advanced exploits and spyware may also be used to gain access to devices.

Such operations generally depend on discovering or purchasing vulnerabilities that can be exploited through links, messages, media files, or in some cases even specially crafted calls.

---

## Key Takeaway

Advanced spyware capable of extracting photos, messages, location data, and other sensitive information does exist and has been used in high-profile investigations. Governments and intelligence agencies are known to possess or acquire zero-day vulnerabilities that can compromise targeted devices under certain circumstances. Some sophisticated exploits may allow access through malicious links, specially crafted messages, or even missed calls when vulnerable software components process the incoming data. These attacks work because attackers know about and exploit software vulnerabilities. Commercial mercenary spyware platforms such as Pegasus have demonstrated how powerful these capabilities can be. At the same time, modern security protections—including Apple's Lockdown Mode—have significantly improved defenses against such attacks. While there is no publicly proven universal backdoor present in every smartphone, reports of governments using sophisticated exploits to gain access to phones are generally consistent with known capabilities demonstrated by advanced surveillance and spyware operations.
