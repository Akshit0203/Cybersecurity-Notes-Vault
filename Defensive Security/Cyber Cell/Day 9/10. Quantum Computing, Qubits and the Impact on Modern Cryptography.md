
# Quantum Computing, Qubits and the Impact on Modern Cryptography

# 1. What is Quantum Computing?

A **Quantum Computer** is a computer that uses the principles of **quantum mechanics** to perform computations.

Unlike classical computers that process information using **bits (0 or 1)**, quantum computers use **qubits**, which can exist in multiple states simultaneously.

---

# 2. Classical Bit vs Quantum Bit

## Classical Bit

A classical bit can only have one value at a time.

```text
0

or

1
```

---

## Quantum Bit (Qubit)

A qubit can exist in a combination of both states until it is measured.

```text
0

1

or

α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
```

This property is called **Superposition**.

---

# 3. How Are Qubits Actually Made?

A qubit is **not** just software—it is a **physical quantum system** that can represent two quantum states.

Different technologies are used to build qubits.

### A. Superconducting Qubits (IBM, Google)

Made using:

- Josephson Junctions
    
- Superconducting circuits
    
- Microwave resonators
    

These operate at temperatures close to **absolute zero (~15–20 millikelvin)** using dilution refrigerators.

---

### B. Trapped Ion Qubits

Use:

- Individual charged atoms (ions)
    
- Electromagnetic fields
    
- Laser pulses
    

Companies:

- IonQ
    
- Quantinuum
    

---

### C. Neutral Atom Qubits

Use:

- Neutral atoms
    
- Optical tweezers
    
- Laser control
    

---

### D. Photonic Qubits

Use:

- Individual photons
    
- Polarization
    
- Phase
    
- Optical waveguides
    

---

### E. Spin Qubits

Use:

- Electron spin
    
- Nuclear spin
    

Research is ongoing for silicon-based quantum processors.

---

# 4. Superposition

Superposition means a qubit can represent multiple probability states simultaneously until measured.

Instead of:

```text
0

OR

1
```

it exists mathematically as:

```text
α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
```

After measurement, it collapses to either **0** or **1**.

---

# 5. Entanglement

Entanglement is a quantum phenomenon where two or more qubits become strongly correlated.

If qubits are entangled:

```text
Qubit A  ←────────→  Qubit B
```

Measuring one affects the joint quantum state of the pair, regardless of physical distance. It does **not** enable faster-than-light communication.

Entanglement enables:

- Quantum algorithms
    
- Quantum teleportation (state transfer)
    
- Quantum error correction
    
- Quantum networking
    

---

# 6. Interdependence of Qubits

In entangled systems, qubits are **interdependent**.

Their quantum states cannot always be described independently.

Example:

```text
Qubit A

↓

Entangled State

↓

Qubit B
```

The system must often be treated as a single quantum state.

---

# 7. Quantum Parallelism

Because of superposition:

```text
1 Qubit  → 2 states

2 Qubits → 4 states

3 Qubits → 8 states

n Qubits → 2ⁿ possible basis states
```

This allows certain quantum algorithms to explore many possibilities more efficiently than classical algorithms.

---

# 8. Quantum Gates

Quantum gates manipulate qubits.

Examples:

- Hadamard (H)
    
- Pauli-X
    
- Pauli-Y
    
- Pauli-Z
    
- CNOT
    
- Toffoli
    

Example circuit:

```text
|0⟩ ──H────●──

           │

|0⟩ ───────X──
```

---

# 9. Why Quantum Computers Threaten Cryptography

Many current public-key cryptosystems rely on mathematical problems that are computationally difficult for classical computers.

Large, fault-tolerant quantum computers running **Shor's Algorithm** could efficiently solve:

- Integer factorization
    
- Discrete logarithms
    

This threatens several widely used public-key algorithms.

---

# 10. RSA and Quantum Computing

RSA security relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers.

```text
RSA

↓

Large Integer Factorization

↓

Shor's Algorithm

↓

Efficient Quantum Solution
```

A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break RSA much faster than known classical methods.

---

# 11. ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography)

ECC security relies on the **Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP).**

Shor's Algorithm would also efficiently solve this problem on a sufficiently capable quantum computer.

Therefore:

- ECC is also considered **quantum-vulnerable**.
    

---

# 12. What About AES?

Symmetric encryption is affected differently.

Quantum computers can use **Grover's Algorithm**, which provides a quadratic speedup for brute-force key search.

Approximate impact:

|AES Version|Classical Security|Effective Quantum Security*|
|---|---|---|
|AES-128|128-bit|~64-bit|
|AES-192|192-bit|~96-bit|
|AES-256|256-bit|~128-bit|

*Approximate effective security against brute-force search using Grover's Algorithm.

**AES-256 is currently considered quantum-resistant enough for long-term planning**, although it still experiences a theoretical reduction in brute-force complexity.

---

# 13. Algorithms Affected

Quantum-vulnerable:

- RSA
    
- Diffie–Hellman
    
- Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)
    
- ECDSA
    
- EdDSA
    

More resilient (with larger key sizes):

- AES
    
- SHA-256 (reduced security margin)
    
- SHA-3
    

---

# 14. Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)

Because RSA and ECC are vulnerable to future quantum attacks, new algorithms are being standardized.

Examples include:

- ML-KEM (formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber)
    
- ML-DSA (formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium)
    
- SLH-DSA (formerly SPHINCS+)
    

These are designed to resist attacks from both classical and quantum computers.

---

# 15. Key Takeaways

- A **qubit** is the fundamental unit of quantum information and is implemented using physical quantum systems such as superconducting circuits, trapped ions, photons, or atomic spins.
    
- **Superposition** allows a qubit to exist in a combination of basis states until measured.
    
- **Entanglement** creates strong quantum correlations between qubits and is fundamental to quantum computation.
    
- **RSA** and **ECC** are expected to be vulnerable to sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computers running **Shor's Algorithm**.
    
- **AES-256** remains one of the strongest practical symmetric encryption algorithms, although Grover's Algorithm theoretically reduces brute-force complexity.
    
- The cybersecurity industry is transitioning toward **Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)** to prepare for the future impact of quantum computing.
