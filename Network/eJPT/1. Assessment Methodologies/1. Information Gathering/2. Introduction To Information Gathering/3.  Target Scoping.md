# Target Scoping

> Defining exactly what you are allowed to test (and collect information about) before beginning any reconnaissance or penetration test.

---
## What Is Target Scoping?

- The process of defining exactly what **systems, networks, or applications** you are allowed to test.
- In a penetration test, you are **not** testing "everything you can find" — you test only what has been **explicitly authorized**.
- The scope is typically laid out in the **Letter of Engagement (LOE)** and more specifically in the **Rules of Engagement (ROE)**.
- In the context of information gathering, scoping answers one simple question:

> **"What am I allowed to collect information about?"**

> [!TIP]
> If you've participated in a **bug bounty**, you've already seen this — programs define in-scope and out-of-scope targets telling you what you can and cannot test.

---
## Common Types of Targets

### 1. Domain-Based Targets

| Element          | Examples                                           |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary domain   | `example.com`                                      |
| Subdomains       | `mail.example.com`, `admin.example.com`            |
| Local domains    | `testsite.local`                                   |

- A domain-based scope may include the **primary domain**, specific **subdomains**, or both.
- Subdomains can also be explicitly **out of scope** — the company specifies exactly which to test.

### 2. IP-Based Targets

| Element        | Examples              |
| -------------- | --------------------- |
| Single IP      | `192.168.1.10`        |
| Network range  | `192.168.1.0/24`      |

- IP-based scopes are common in **internal**, **lab-based**, or **cloud-based** environments.
- A network range means you can test any system within that range.

### 3. Application-Based Targets

| Element              | Examples                                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Web application      | A specific web app or mobile application   |
| Specific endpoint    | A login portal, admin panel, or API endpoint |

- Reconnaissance focuses on **that application/endpoint only**, not the entire server or web application.

---
## In-Scope vs. Out-of-Scope

| | In-Scope | Out-of-Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | Assets you **are allowed** to interact with | Assets you **must not** interact with |
| **Actions** | Collect information from, scan, enumerate | Do not touch — completely off-limits |
| **Examples** | Listed domains, IPs, applications | Third-party services, external domains not listed, systems owned by another organization |

---
## Why Scoping Matters

> Reconnaissance is about collecting **useful** information, not **all** information.

**Without proper scoping:**
- You may scan irrelevant hosts → wasting time
- You may miss the actual target
- Results become noisy and confusing
- It essentially turns into an unstructured black-box / red-team operation

**A well-defined scope keeps reconnaissance:**
- ✅ Focused
- ✅ Efficient
- ✅ Relevant to later stages

> [!CAUTION]
> **Scope is legally binding.** Once you sign the LOE and Rules of Engagement, you **cannot** test anything outside the defined scope. Going out of scope can make you **liable for any damage** caused and constitutes a breach of the engagement terms. If the company isn't sure about scope, it's your responsibility to help them define it clearly before the engagement begins.
